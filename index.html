Course example 01

Quantitative reasoning through meaningful scenarios

Critical Teaching Behavior: INCLUDE

How can we make college algebra more inclusive and engaging while simultaneously raising expectations for students' mathematical reasoning and communication?

In this redesigned College Algebra course, major timed examinations were replaced with weekly quantitative reasoning projects. Students had a full week to investigate a meaningful scenario and construct an argument using the Claim–Evidence–Reasoning (CER) framework. The emphasis shifted from performing procedures under time pressure toward developing, defending, and communicating mathematical understanding. Rigor remained central: students were expected to demonstrate deep conceptual understanding in their reports, while separate mastery requirements ensured proficiency in essential symbolic skills.

Inclusion was built into the course through flexibility, relevance, and participation. Students could select from instructor-provided scenarios or pursue examples connected to their own academic, professional, or personal interests. The first three weeks provided scaffolding in CER argumentation, preparing students for increasingly sophisticated work throughout the semester.

Students also became active contributors to one another's learning. Each Friday, several students presented their findings, while their classmates reflected on the mathematical arguments presented. This approach positioned students not merely as recipients of instruction, but as participants in the teaching process.

Ultimately, the redesign sought to broaden access to meaningful mathematical learning without sacrificing rigor. By emphasizing student choice, collaborative learning, and written and oral communication, the course also connected quantitative reasoning to skills students will need beyond the classroom and in their future professions.

Learning design and AI

AI supported both the design and delivery of the course, but the central learning objective remained distinctly human: interpreting quantitative information, constructing defensible arguments, and communicating mathematical understanding.

AI-supported scenario development

Each weekly quantitative reasoning project offered students three instructor-provided scenarios, often drawn from real-world news, research, or professional contexts. Generative AI dramatically reduced the time required to locate relevant, engaging scenarios for each mathematical topic. Students could also identify their own scenarios, allowing them to connect course concepts to their academic programs, professional aspirations, or personal interests.

AI also helped generate a large collection of fictional but plausible micro-scenarios for classroom instruction. Unlike the authentic sources used for reports, these short examples did not need to correspond to actual events. Their purpose was to make mathematical ideas accessible through recognizable situations.

Scaffolding reasoning and communication

Instruction followed a weekly rhythm designed to prepare students for independent mathematical argumentation:

  • Monday and Wednesday: The instructor and students worked through AI-generated micro-scenarios, practicing how to develop claims, identify quantitative evidence, and explain the reasoning connecting them.
  • Throughout the week: Students independently investigated a chosen scenario and prepared a written report using the Claim–Evidence–Reasoning (CER) framework.
  • Friday: Three to five students presented their arguments to the class. Their peers completed reflections on each presentation, evaluating and learning from one another's mathematical reasoning.

The first three weeks emphasized learning the CER framework through relatively simple mathematical topics. As students became comfortable with the format, subsequent projects demanded increasingly sophisticated applications of college algebra.

Oral presentations and academic integrity

Oral presentations also served a deliberate academic integrity purpose. In The Opposite of Cheating (Bertram Gallant & Rettinger, 2025), the authors emphasize the influence of perceived peer behavior on students' decisions to cheat: students who believe their classmates are cheating are themselves more likely to cheat. Drawing on the principles of oral assessment, the course incorporated live presentations in which students had to demonstrate and communicate their mathematical understanding before their peers. Because such performances are difficult to outsource convincingly, their visible presence may also strengthen students' confidence that their classmates are doing authentic work. Thus, oral presentations were intended not only to develop communication skills but also to cultivate a classroom culture of academic integrity.

Reconsidering assessment in the age of AI

Generative AI can readily solve many of the routine symbolic problems traditionally emphasized in introductory mathematics courses. This raises an important assessment question:

If students can obtain procedural solutions almost instantly, what mathematical abilities should our assessments prioritize?

The course responded by shifting its major assessments from timed procedural performance toward interpretation, application, and communication. Students were permitted to use AI to explore scenarios, investigate mathematical approaches, and refine their arguments, but their work was evaluated on the quality of the mathematical reasoning they presented. Separate mastery requirements preserved expectations for essential symbolic proficiency.

Rather than attempting to eliminate AI from the learning process, the redesign emphasized students' responsibility to make sense of mathematical results, evaluate their significance, and defend their conclusions.

Teaching First, AI Second: AI made meaningful, individualized quantitative reasoning activities more feasible to develop and teach. The learning goal remained students' ability to construct and communicate defensible mathematical arguments within a classroom culture that valued authentic learning and academic integrity.

Evidence and reflection

Gathering evidence through Break Reflections

In addition to weekly reflections on their classmates' presentations, students completed two more substantial Break Reflections, approximately five and nine weeks into the semester. These occurred during weeks without quantitative reasoning reports or presentations, providing students an opportunity to reflect on their developing mathematical understanding.

The Break Reflections were deliberately low-stakes. Students received full credit for following the requested structure, and the two assignments together accounted for just 3% of the final course grade. The intent was to encourage candid reflection rather than reward particular attitudes or conclusions.

Across two course sections, students submitted 92 Break Reflections at the two checkpoints: 46 at each. The analysis presented here focuses on these assignments rather than the weekly presentation reflections.

Students increasingly described mathematics as meaning-making

A recurring theme was students' growing recognition that mathematics involves interpreting quantities and relationships, not merely executing symbolic procedures. Students frequently contrasted their previous emphasis on obtaining correct numerical answers with their developing attention to context, assumptions, and mathematical meaning.

One student captured this shift particularly well:

“Having that connection... brought me behind the veil of what the numbers mean.”

The qualitative findings identified four interconnected themes:

  • Units as interpretive tools: Students recognized that numbers require units and context to convey meaning.
  • Assumptions as part of modeling: Students increasingly understood that mathematical models depend on assumptions that establish their applicability and limitations.
  • Mathematics as relational: Students described equations, graphs, and rates of change as representations of relationships between quantities.
  • Changing mathematical habits: Students reported moving beyond a focus on procedural completion toward interpreting results and considering their implications.

These themes are explored further in the Qualitative Findings Table, including representative student excerpts and pedagogical implications.

What we learned

The reflections suggest an encouraging shift in how students understood the purpose of mathematics. Students increasingly articulated the importance of interpretation, communication, and contextual reasoning—the very abilities emphasized by the redesigned assessments.

Importantly, this development was not uniform or complete. Some students recognized the importance of mathematical meaning while continuing to struggle with verbal explanations or identifying appropriate assumptions.

These findings describe students' emerging perspectives on mathematics, rather than independently demonstrating improved mathematical proficiency or establishing that AI caused changes in learning. Nevertheless, they provide encouraging evidence that an assessment model emphasizing meaningful applications and communication can support students in reconsidering what it means to understand mathematics.

Course example 02

Economics Through AI: From Concepts to Creative Application

Course focus

Critical Teaching Behavior: ENGAGE

Instructors who engage students purposefully select research-based techniques to ensure that students actively participate in the learning process and take responsibility for their intellectual development.

How can we move students beyond memorizing economic terminology to actively applying economic concepts in ways that are meaningful, relevant, and engaging?

In an undergraduate Macroeconomics course, AI was incorporated as both a learning tool and a creative medium. The goal was to help students explore economic relationships, connect theory to real-world situations, and communicate their understanding in new ways—not simply to teach them how to use AI.

The course emphasized interpreting economic indicators, analyzing supply and demand, applying macroeconomic models, evaluating economic policies, and examining how economic conditions influence businesses and everyday decisions.

Learning design and AI

Students encountered AI through learning resources and activities designed to encourage exploration, application, and communication. They translated economic concepts into music, industry analysis, and digital communication.

Narrating Markets Through Music | Suno AI

Students created original songs explaining economic concepts through recognizable market situations. Using Suno, they transformed ideas such as supply, demand, scarcity, and price changes into lyrics and music. They had to develop a scenario, explain the economic relationship, and communicate it creatively—not just define terms.

Lemonade Stand: Supply and Demand
In this student submission, hot weather increases demand for lemonade, creating a shortage at the original price and putting upward pressure on the equilibrium price. Listen to the student song.

Final Business Economics Briefing | Lovable AI

Students selected an industry and developed a web-based economic briefing using Lovable, an AI-supported website-building platform. Their briefings brought together GDP, inflation, unemployment, aggregate demand and supply, fiscal and monetary policy, and global economic influences. The aim was to communicate an economically informed industry analysis to an audience beyond the classroom.

AI-Supported Industry Economic Briefing: Students created interactive webpages connecting macroeconomic indicators, policy, and industry conditions. View a student-created economic briefing.

Additional learning activities

Interactive economic infographics, economic memes, and AI-supported study resources offered multiple ways to engage with course material while keeping economic reasoning at the center.

Evidence and reflection

67% demonstrated economic application

A retrospective review found that 12 of 18 distinct Suno assignment submissions (66.7%) demonstrated real-world application and substantially accurate economic reasoning.

Submissions were assessed for three elements:

  • Identification of an economic concept.
  • Application of that concept to a recognizable real-world situation.
  • A substantially accurate explanation of the economic relationship.

Two submissions did not provide sufficient accessible written evidence to evaluate economic accuracy. The reported percentage includes all 18 distinct submissions in its denominator.

Applied learning through AI-generated webpages

The final business briefing gave students another opportunity to connect economic indicators, policy decisions, and global forces to specific industries. Of 21 submission records, three uploaded documents were available for substantive review. All three demonstrated identifiable economic concepts, industry applications, and explanations of economic relationships. The remaining webpage-link submissions were not sufficiently accessible for comparable assessment, so no overall webpage application rate is reported.

Student reflections

Eighteen end-of-course reflections offered insight into students’ experiences. Students described connections between economics and current events, everyday decisions, and professional interests. These reflections support the broader relevance of the course experience; they do not establish that AI itself caused increased engagement.

What we learned

AI can offer alternative ways for students to engage with disciplinary content without replacing the disciplinary thinking that matters. Students translated concepts into music, industry analysis, and digital communication, while economic reasoning remained the instructional priority. These results are descriptive evidence of student application in an AI-supported course, not evidence of causal improvement attributable to AI.

Implications for teaching

  • Begin with the learning objective and select AI tools that serve it.
  • Use creative assignments that require students to explain and apply disciplinary concepts, not simply generate polished products.
  • Incorporate explicit reflection and consistent assessment criteria to distinguish engagement, conceptual accuracy, and AI’s contribution.

Teaching First, AI Second: AI served as a medium for engagement. The learning goal remained the meaningful application of economics.

Course example 03

Rhetorical discernment in AI-assisted business writing

Course focus

Critical Teaching Behavior: ALIGN

Instructors who align components of learning experiences start with clear learning goals. Measurable outcomes, teaching and learning activities, assessment tasks, and feedback build on each other to support student progress toward those goals.

Course design: backward design as alignment in practice

This course was built backward, beginning with one central learning outcome: Use generative AI strategically across the writing process and improve drafts using human judgment.

Next came the evidence: a semester-long, scaffolded marketing campaign project with four milestones, designed to make student thinking about AI visible and assessable. Decisions about how AI would be introduced, practiced, and reflected upon at each stage followed. This sequence—outcome, evidence, instruction—is backward design and alignment in practice.

Learning design and AI

Giving students a language: the AI Use Scale

To support rhetorical discernment about AI, students worked with the Student AI Use Scale (University of Kentucky, 2025), a five-level continuum ranging from Sole Author (Level 0) to Project Manager (Level 4). Each marketing-project milestone specified an appropriate level of AI use, giving students a concrete vocabulary for making and justifying choices about when and how to use AI—and when not to.

This was not a policy. It was a pedagogical scaffold.

The artifacts

  • AI-Powered Collaborative Marketing Campaign Project: Four-milestone semester project integrating AI-use instruction at each stage.
  • Student AI Use Scale: Shared framework for rhetorical decision-making about AI.
  • Individual In-Class Metacognitive Reflection: End-of-semester reflection on students’ AI use, writing development, and growth toward course learning outcomes.

Evidence of student learning

Student metacognitive reflections were coded for rhetorical discernment: the ability to explain why an AI output was useful, what was changed, or what was done instead. Two-thirds of students—enrolled in their first AI-enhanced class ever—demonstrated operational fluency, articulating reasoned judgments about AI use grounded in audience, purpose, and genre.
