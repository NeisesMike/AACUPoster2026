Course example 01

Quantitative reasoning through meaningful scenarios

Course focus

Boost student engagement by aligning course content and assessments with the general education requirement in Quantitative Reasoning and students’ academic programs, weighted by representation.

Learning outcomes: Make defensible claims, communicate quantitative reasoning clearly, and evaluate and summarize quantitative arguments made by others.

Learning design and AI

Students worked with instructor-curated, AI-generated scenarios, often aligned with academic programs. They made a claim about a scenario and supported it with evidence and reasoning using the Claim–Evidence–Reasoning (CER) framework.

Students could use AI to explore a scenario, find a more engaging scenario, and refine their argument. Oral presentations were also required; the instructor notes that this may reduce pressure to cheat.

Evidence and reflection

Milestone reflections suggest students recognized mathematics as meaning-making, not just executing procedures. Nearly all students reflected on meaning-making. One student wrote, “Having that connection... brought me behind the veil of what the numbers mean.”

The reported emotional response was described as “transformative, relieved, and empowered.”

Course example 02

Economics Through AI: From Concepts to Creative Application

Course focus

Critical Teaching Behavior: ENGAGE

How can we move students beyond memorizing economic terminology to actively applying economic concepts in ways that are meaningful, relevant, and engaging?

In an undergraduate Macroeconomics course, AI was incorporated as both a learning tool and a creative medium. The goal was to help students explore economic relationships, connect theory to real-world situations, and communicate their understanding in new ways—not simply to teach them how to use AI.

The course emphasized interpreting economic indicators, analyzing supply and demand, applying macroeconomic models, evaluating economic policies, and examining how economic conditions influence businesses and everyday decisions.

Learning design and AI

Students encountered AI through learning resources and activities designed to encourage exploration, application, and communication. They translated economic concepts into music, industry analysis, and digital communication.

Narrating Markets Through Music | Suno AI

Students created original songs explaining economic concepts through recognizable market situations. Using Suno, they transformed ideas such as supply, demand, scarcity, and price changes into lyrics and music. They had to develop a scenario, explain the economic relationship, and communicate it creatively—not just define terms.

Lemonade Stand: Supply and Demand
In this student submission, hot weather increases demand for lemonade, creating a shortage at the original price and putting upward pressure on the equilibrium price. Listen to the student song.

Final Business Economics Briefing | Lovable AI

Students selected an industry and developed a web-based economic briefing using Lovable, an AI-supported website-building platform. Their briefings brought together GDP, inflation, unemployment, aggregate demand and supply, fiscal and monetary policy, and global economic influences. The aim was to communicate an economically informed industry analysis to an audience beyond the classroom.

AI-Supported Industry Economic Briefing: Students created interactive webpages connecting macroeconomic indicators, policy, and industry conditions. View a student-created economic briefing (student webpage links are not included because they were not sufficiently accessible for review).

Additional learning activities

Interactive economic infographics, economic memes, and AI-supported study resources offered multiple ways to engage with course material while keeping economic reasoning at the center.

Evidence and reflection

67% demonstrated economic application

A retrospective review found that 12 of 18 distinct Suno assignment submissions (66.7%) demonstrated real-world application and substantially accurate economic reasoning.

Submissions were assessed for three elements:

  • Identification of an economic concept.
  • Application of that concept to a recognizable real-world situation.
  • A substantially accurate explanation of the economic relationship.

Two submissions did not provide sufficient accessible written evidence to evaluate economic accuracy. The reported percentage includes all 18 distinct submissions in its denominator.

Applied learning through AI-generated webpages

The final business briefing gave students another opportunity to connect economic indicators, policy decisions, and global forces to specific industries. Of 21 submission records, three uploaded documents were available for substantive review. All three demonstrated identifiable economic concepts, industry applications, and explanations of economic relationships. The remaining webpage-link submissions were not sufficiently accessible for comparable assessment, so no overall webpage application rate is reported.

Student reflections

Eighteen end-of-course reflections offered insight into students’ experiences. Students described connections between economics and current events, everyday decisions, and professional interests. These reflections support the broader relevance of the course experience; they do not establish that AI itself caused increased engagement.

What we learned

AI can offer alternative ways for students to engage with disciplinary content without replacing the disciplinary thinking that matters. Students translated concepts into music, industry analysis, and digital communication, while economic reasoning remained the instructional priority. These results are descriptive evidence of student application in an AI-supported course, not evidence of causal improvement attributable to AI.

Implications for teaching

  • Begin with the learning objective and select AI tools that serve it.
  • Use creative assignments that require students to explain and apply disciplinary concepts, not simply generate polished products.
  • Incorporate explicit reflection and consistent assessment criteria to distinguish engagement, conceptual accuracy, and AI’s contribution.

Course example 03

Rhetorical discernment in AI-assisted business writing

Course focus

Develop students’ rhetorical discernment through course design that aligns AI-related learning outcomes, assessments, and activities.

Learning outcome: Use AI strategically during the writing process and improve drafts through human judgment.

Learning design and AI

Across semester-long group marketing campaigns, students tested AI output against the rhetorical context and decided whether to accept, change, or reject it.

A student AI-use scale, adapted from Perkins and Ferze (2024), was used in TILTed assignments to support discernment and agency. The source notes that students moved among different roles while using AI.
