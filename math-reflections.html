Analysis

Quantitative Findings: ENGAGE Dimensions

Overview

The valid reflection corpus consisted of 92 submissions from 48 unique students across two sections and two reflection periods. Reflections were coded for three dimensions associated with the ENGAGE critical teaching behavior framework:

  1. Engagement via authentic/applied tasks
  2. Engagement via peer interaction and presentations
  3. Engagement via metacognitive reflection

Because the students generally did not use the formal term Critical Teaching Behaviors, the analysis identifies explicit evidence of these dimensions in students’ descriptions of their learning experiences.

Synthesis of Findings

The strongest and most consistently documented ENGAGE dimension was authentic/applied task engagement. Students repeatedly connected mathematical ideas to situations beyond routine classroom exercises, including fuel efficiency, medical dosage plans, phone plans, travel time, aquifer depletion, population change, taxation, income, shopping, and compound interest. These connections suggest that applied scenarios helped students view mathematics as useful for interpreting decisions and consequences in everyday, professional, and civic contexts.

Metacognitive reflection was also highly prevalent. Students commonly identified specific difficulties—such as interpreting units, distinguishing linear from exponential growth, scaling graphs, connecting representations, identifying assumptions, or explaining slope and intercepts—and paired those difficulties with plans for improvement. The reflections therefore provide evidence not only of engagement with course content but also of self-monitoring and self-regulation.

Evidence for peer interaction and presentations was less frequent in the written reflections. This lower count should not automatically be interpreted as evidence that peer interaction was ineffective. The reflection prompts emphasized mathematical content, applications, challenges, and improvement plans; they did not consistently ask students to evaluate peer learning or classroom presentations. Consequently, peer engagement was likely under-recorded relative to authentic-task and metacognitive engagement.

Discussion

Taken together, the findings indicate that the course design engaged students primarily through contextualized mathematical work and structured reflection on learning. The high prevalence of authentic/applied references suggests that students recognized real-world contexts as meaningful settings for mathematical reasoning rather than as decorative examples added after procedural instruction.

The metacognitive findings reinforce this interpretation. Students did not simply report whether they understood a topic; many analyzed how they were thinking, identified where their reasoning broke down, and proposed concrete adjustments. Examples included checking units, slowing down before calculating, writing assumptions, practicing a specific method, explaining rates in complete sentences, and connecting equations, tables, graphs, and contexts.

The peer-interaction finding requires the greatest caution. Only a small number of students spontaneously mentioned peer presentations or classmates’ work. This result supports the claim that peer engagement was explicitly visible in the reflections of some students, but it does not establish that peer interaction occurred for only 6 students or that it had little instructional value. A future reflection prompt could ask directly:

“Describe one idea, strategy, or representation you learned from a classmate’s presentation or discussion. How did it influence your understanding?”

Such a prompt would provide a more valid basis for estimating the prevalence and impact of peer-mediated engagement.

Coding was conducted with AI assistance through iterative review of the reflection files. For the meaning-making analysis, a response qualified when it explicitly characterized mathematics as involving interpretation, context, units, assumptions, relationships, consequences, or real-world meaning—particularly when it contrasted these practices with merely obtaining an answer, applying a formula, or following procedures. For the ENGAGE analysis, responses were coded for explicit references to: (1) authentic or applied mathematical tasks, (2) peer interaction or presentations, and (3) metacognitive reflection, defined as identifying one’s own difficulty, misconception, or learning process and describing a strategy for improvement. A response could receive more than one code.

Ambiguous cases were handled conservatively: a theme was counted only when the relevant idea was explicitly stated or clearly paraphrased by the student. Responses that merely defined a mathematical term or listed procedural steps without reflective or contextual commentary were not coded as meaning-making. Similarly, peer engagement was counted only when students explicitly mentioned classmates, presentations, or peer learning; it was not inferred from the collaborative structure of an assignment. All available entries were first inventoried, after which empty or incomplete entries were excluded from the valid-submission denominator.

These results should be understood as AI-assisted interpretive coding, not as automated measurement. For publication, the coding decisions, quotations, exclusions, and final counts should be audited against the original files by the instructor or a second human coder. In particular, the reported percentages should be retained only after confirming that every response assigned to each category satisfies the stated inclusion criteria.
