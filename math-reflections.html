Analysis

Qualitative findings table

Emergent themes, operational definitions, student excerpts, and pedagogical implications
Emergent Theme Operational Definition Exemplary Student Excerpts (De-identified Identifiers) Pedagogical Implication
1. Units functioned as interpretive tools Students increasingly treated units as essential for determining what a numerical quantity represents, evaluating whether an answer is reasonable, and connecting symbolic work to real-world situations. Units were understood as more than labels attached after calculation.

Section A, Break 1 — Response A1: “Numbers alone are kind of empty. They need units to have real meaning.”

Section A, Break 1 — Response A2: “Numbers don’t mean much without context. Units … help you understand what’s actually going on, and assumptions make problems easier to solve.”

Section B, Break 1 — Response B1: “The only way the numbers make sense is if we have the right units.”

 Incorporate dimensional analysis and unit interpretation throughout problem solving, rather than treating units as a final formatting requirement. Require students to state what each quantity measures and to use units to check the plausibility of their answers.
2. Assumptions became visible as part of mathematical modeling Students recognized that assumptions are conditions or simplifications that make a model possible but also establish its limitations. They began to understand that a mathematically correct calculation may still be inappropriate if the underlying assumptions are unrealistic.

Section B, Break 1 — Response B2: “Assumptions are not just ‘guesses,’ but critical constraints that define the limits of the model’s accuracy.”

Section B, Break 1 — Response B3: “If the units or assumptions are wrong, the math might still work, but the answer wouldn’t make sense in real life.”

Section A, Break 1 — Response A3: “Assumptions aren’t extra details; they are a huge part of math.”

 Ask students to identify, justify, and critique assumptions before solving applied problems. Follow calculations with questions such as: “What had to be true for this model to work?” and “How would the answer change if that assumption changed?”
3. Mathematics was increasingly understood as relational Students described mathematics as a way to represent relationships among quantities, including rates of change, dependencies, comparisons, patterns, and trade-offs. Equations, tables, and graphs were interpreted as representations of how quantities change together.

Section A, Break 1 — Response A4: “I see it is really comparison between two things that change together in steady way.” The response also described slope as “telling a story.”

Section A, Break 1 — Response A5: “I learned that slope represents a comparison between two quantities … not just ‘rise over run.’”

Section B, Break 2 — Response B4: “Across all these topics, I noticed that math is about recognizing patterns and relationships.”

 Design tasks that require students to move among equations, tables, graphs, and written interpretations. Emphasize questions about how one quantity changes in relation to another, rather than focusing exclusively on obtaining numerical answers.
4. Students described changes in their mathematical identities and habits of mind Students portrayed a shift from rushing toward an answer, memorizing formulas, or focusing only on procedural completion toward attending to meaning, context, assumptions, and consequences. The reflections also indicate that this shift was emerging rather than uniformly mastered.

Section B, Break 1 — Response B5: “Before this class, I mostly focused on getting the right number, but now I understand that the meaning behind the number depends on the units attached to it.”

Section B, Break 1 — Response B6: “I used to focus only on getting the right answer. Now I think more about meaning, context, and consequences.”

Section B, Break 1 — Response B7: “I struggle with understanding rather than solving and doing the steps to get an answer. To improve on this I will look more into the bigger picture and find what everything means.”

 Treat reflection and explanation as evidence of mathematical learning, not as peripheral activities. Continue using prompts that ask students to describe changes in their thinking, identify persistent difficulties, and articulate specific strategies for improvement.

Discussion

Across the reflections, students articulated a broadening conception of quantitative reasoning. They increasingly described units as sources of meaning, assumptions as components of mathematical models, and equations and graphs as representations of relationships among quantities. Many also reported a shift in their mathematical habits—from pursuing an answer procedurally toward interpreting results in context and considering their consequences.

The fourth theme is stated cautiously. The reflections provide strong evidence of emerging changes in mathematical identity and orientation, but they do not establish that every student had fully replaced procedural thinking with conceptual reasoning. Several students explicitly recognized the importance of meaning while also identifying verbal interpretation, assumption-setting, or contextual application as continuing areas for development.
